Brain Modelling

High-fidelity computational models of brain biomechanics, used to predict the effects of mechanical loading on brain tissue.

What we study

We develop high fidelity computational models of brain biomechanics and use them to predict the effects of mechanical loading on brain tissue. Our work centres on the Imperial College Finite Element Model of Brain Injury Biomechanics, which incorporates fine details of brain anatomy — including sulci, white-matter tracts, deep structures, and cerebral vasculature down to capillary scale.

The model has identified the location of pathology in chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) and revealed the biomechanical signature of loss of consciousness in elite athletes. It now underpins much of the lab’s other work — helmet rating, exposure monitoring, and clinical collaborations.

Why it matters

Most head impacts don’t injure the skull — they injure the brain. The response that matters — diffuse axonal injury, subdural bleeds, cavitation, microbleeds — happens at scales and locations that current tests and rating systems don’t capture. Models give us a way to see what helmets and rigs miss, and to design what to do about it. Validated brain models are central to the lab’s contributions to helmet standards (CEN/TC158/WG11, EN17950), to World Rugby’s return-to-play guidelines, and to the MRC TBI-REPORTER national data platform.

How we do it

Our modelling work runs on several tracks:

  • The Imperial College FE brain model, validated against post-mortem human-subject experiments and used across the lab’s projects.
  • Biomechanics of axonal and vascular injury — connecting white-matter and vessel deformation to long-term outcomes after TBI.
  • Subject-specific (individualised) brain models built from MRI via a semi-automatic pipeline, in hours rather than weeks.
  • From biomechanics to pathology — translational work linking mechanical loading to histological changes weeks after injury, in collaboration with neuroscientists at Imperial’s Brain Sciences Department, the Centre for Blast Injury Studies, and clinicians at Imperial Neurotrauma Centre.
  • Surrogate models — fast machine-learning approximations of the FE model, suitable for near-real-time use with wearable sensors.

This work has been funded by Wellcome Trust Networks of Excellence, Wellcome Trust Seed Awards in Science, EPSRC DTP studentships, the Royal Academy of Engineering Senior Research Fellowship, and the Centre for Blast Injury Studies.
